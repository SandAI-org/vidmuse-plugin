# Product launch film direction

Use this reference for product launches, website promos, SaaS films, feature
announcements, and brand sizzles. It is a directorial brief for the model, not
a checklist or approval system.

## Outcome

Create a short film in which the product feels like an event unfolding in
time, not a sequence of decorated screenshots.

The viewer should experience:

1. a compelling change or promise;
2. the product making that change tangible;
3. proof through real interface, output, or product material;
4. an expansion in possibility, scale, or emotional energy;
5. one memorable brand resolution and CTA.

Start from the moment the audience cares about, not the feature list. A launch
film gives the product a context and a transformation to own.

## Choose one directorial idea

Write one sentence that describes the film-wide action. Good ideas contain a
verb and a change:

- a signal enters the product and expands into many finished worlds;
- a crowded workflow collapses into one clear creative gesture;
- the camera travels from a tiny input detail to a vast field of outcomes;
- one product surface changes scale and becomes the environment around it.

Use that action to decide composition, camera, transitions, and asset roles.
Do not use the concept merely as opening copy and then return to unrelated
feature cards.

## Build flow, not pages

Treat adjacent scenes as neighboring moments in one visual world.

- Carry motion, direction, scale, color energy, an object, or a framing edge
  across the cut.
- Let the outgoing action motivate the incoming shot. A card moving right can
  become a rightward push; a zoom into UI can emerge inside its result; a light
  sweep can reveal the next material.
- Preserve useful object identity. When the same UI, image, logo, or input
  continues to matter, transform or recompose it instead of hiding it and
  recreating a new slide.
- Change shot size and camera relationship across the film. Alternate macro
  detail, medium product proof, wide outcome space, oblique depth, and quiet
  lockup according to the story.
- Use fewer, richer scenes. A 20–40 second launch film often needs only 4–6
  major scenes when each scene develops internally.

A shared background color is art direction, not continuity by itself.

## Camera grammar

Give each scene a start position, an end position, and a reason for the move.
The viewer should feel where the camera is in relation to the product.

Useful camera actions include:

- push in to turn attention into discovery;
- pull back to reveal scale or many outcomes;
- track across a workflow or media field;
- orbit or tilt a product plane to give it physical presence;
- rack visual focus between foreground detail and background context;
- zoom through a meaningful surface into the result it produces;
- hold the camera when the product action itself is the spectacle.

Move a shared parent when the camera moves. Move media inside its wrapper when
the image itself pans or zooms. Combining those two layers creates depth;
scaling every card independently does not.

Avoid constant-speed travel. Product films usually feel premium when motion
accelerates decisively, settles cleanly, and preserves velocity through a
transition.

## Motion choreography

Think in three simultaneous scales:

1. **Primary motion** changes the scene: camera travel, product transformation,
   reveal, collapse, expansion, or handoff.
2. **Secondary motion** keeps the world alive: media pan, parallax, interface
   response, light movement, depth shift, or restrained audio response.
3. **Detail motion** sharpens emphasis: type assembly, line draw, cursor,
   button state, label, logo, or small accent.

Do not front-load every animation and then freeze the composition. Let the
primary action develop across the scene. During a reading window, keep the
copy stable while relevant media, depth, or camera energy continues quietly
underneath.

Vary entrances by meaning:

- reveal a surface with a mask or crop;
- bring evidence forward through depth;
- assemble related items through stagger and shared momentum;
- let typography respond to a musical hit;
- use a physical handoff when one object causes the next scene.

Motion should explain relationships. Decorative drift, periodic breathing,
and generic fade-up helpers make the film feel templated.

## Transitions

Transitions connect actions; they are not ornaments placed between completed
slides.

- Match the direction and velocity of outgoing and incoming motion.
- Keep transitions short enough that the viewer reads one continuous action.
- Choose a small family that belongs to the product and vary its expression.
- Use cuts when a decisive contrast is the point.
- Use push, wipe, pan, or whip when lateral energy already exists.
- Use zoom-through or scale match when the story changes magnitude.
- Use light, blur, texture, or color leakage when the brand material motivates
  it.
- Use object handoff or graphic match when a shape can persist across scenes.

Prefer two to four related transition ideas across a short film. Richness
comes from motivated variation, not from using a different preset at every
seam.

## Use product material naturally

Real product evidence should become part of the composition rather than sit
inside a presentation frame.

- Inspect all captured images, videos, logos, fonts, and interface states
  before selecting hero material.
- Prefer moving source material when it expresses the product well.
- Crop screenshots to the detail the viewer needs; show the full interface
  only when orientation matters.
- Treat a screenshot as a plane, environment, texture, isolated detail, or
  layered proof surface.
- Use foreground crops and partial off-frame placement to create depth.
- Animate media inside clipped wrappers with restrained pan or zoom.
- Allow one hero asset to dominate; use supporting assets to widen the world,
  not compete at equal scale.
- Reuse a strong asset when repetition creates recognition, but change its
  crop, scale, context, or role.

Avoid a row of equal cards, repeated browser frames, or a thumbnail wall with
no focal hierarchy. Those arrangements describe a library; they do not direct
attention.

## Composition and typography

Design every frame as an editorial composition with one clear focal subject.

- Use asymmetry, scale contrast, overlap, cropping, and depth to create a
  visual path.
- Let important media or type cross conventional grid boundaries when the
  brand supports it.
- Separate message, evidence, and metadata by scale and position.
- Keep copy short enough to read while motion continues.
- Use large typography as an image, not as a heading above a screenshot.
- Preserve deliberate negative space, but avoid large empty regions caused by
  undersized centered content.
- Make random paused frames feel like strong posters, while ensuring the film
  between them still contains meaningful motion.

## Music and sound

No voiceover does not mean silent.

For a launch film longer than roughly ten seconds, use a music bed unless the
user explicitly requests silence. Let musical sections shape the edit:

- hook or inhale;
- first rhythmic entry;
- product proof;
- widest or most energetic section;
- drop, resolve, and logo tail.

Time major visual changes to musical phrases and downbeats, not every minor
element. Use restrained SFX for causal moments such as submit, lock, impact,
transition, or logo resolution when they improve clarity.

## Pacing

- Establish intrigue or the central promise in the first one to three seconds.
- Show credible product proof early enough that the film cannot be mistaken
  for a generic mood reel.
- Alternate compression and release: fast assembly followed by a readable
  reveal; macro detail followed by a wide result; dense proof followed by a
  quiet lockup.
- Let shot length follow information and musical phrasing rather than dividing
  the runtime evenly.
- End with one idea, one brand, and one action. Do not turn the final card into
  a footer full of competing details.

## Translate direction into HyperFrames

- Build the most legible frame of each scene first, then animate toward and
  through it.
- Use one root timeline so scene motion and transitions can overlap.
- Keep a persistent world or shared object outside per-scene containers when
  continuity depends on it.
- Give important still images nested motion: wrapper for camera/depth, media
  element for internal pan or zoom.
- Let outgoing scenes remain visually complete until the transition takes
  control.
- Use video as muted picture plus a separate audio element when its sound is
  needed.
- Keep motion deterministic and seek-safe.

## Directorial review

Watch the result at normal speed and revise from the viewer's experience:

- Does the film still communicate a transformation when the copy is ignored?
- Can you describe the camera path through the whole film?
- Does each transition continue an action or merely replace a layout?
- During readable moments, is the world alive without distracting from copy?
- Are real assets integrated as cinematic material rather than screenshots in
  boxes?
- Do shot scale, density, and energy change enough to create progression?
- Does the ending feel earned by the motion and story that came before it?

If the answer is weak, improve the direction or choreography rather than
adding more decorative effects.
