# Device Craft: Progress, Data, Demonstration

Craft rules for three recurring device families. Each section is
principle-first; anchors are starting points with the standard deviation
clause (depart with a written reason in the self-audit). Charter dimensions:
More convincing (6), Clearer (5), Undisturbed (8) —
[aesthetic-charter.md](aesthetic-charter.md).

## Progress and chapter indexes

### The progress rail is default-on

A quiet time-progress rail is standing orientation chrome for every
packaging run: viewers are measurably more comfortable knowing where they
are in a film, and a plain rail promises only elapsed time — which is always
true, so it costs nothing against honesty. It ships by default; dropping it
requires a written reason in the self-audit (user opt-out, a very short clip
where any persistent chrome reads as noise, or a deliberately cinematic
quiet piece), never silence.

### Chapter marks must have the structure they promise

Chapter chrome — segment ticks, labels, numbered indexes (01/02/03) — is a
promise that the argument has real sequential structure. The test: could you
name each chapter in three words such that the speaker would agree with the
names? When the test **passes**, the preferred form is chapter marks on the
progress rail itself — segments, ticks, or short labels — one system
carrying both orientation and structure, and each confirmed chapter boundary
defaults to a marked transition
([camera-and-transition-craft.md](camera-and-transition-craft.md)). When the
test **fails**, the rail stays plain (time only): manufactured chapter marks
are decorative structure, tell **V6** in
[packaging-tells.md](packaging-tells.md).

### Quiet, consistent, truthful

The rail is orientation, not decoration: it lives at the chrome layer of the
treatment recipe (`ground · composes · focal · chrome · accent`), styled from
FRAME tokens, and never competes with the focal element. Chapter marks
update at chapter boundaries; the rail never animates for its own sake. Its
state always tells the truth — a "3/5" that doesn't match the actual act
structure is fake precision (tell **V3**).

*Anchor:* one continuous index system per film, maximum — a chaptered rail
counts as that one system. A progress rail *and* separate chapter numbers
*and* a section title card is three systems saying one thing.

## Data and chart moments

### Numbers come from the transcript — nowhere else

The Numerals & Claims hard rule applies with no exceptions: every digit on
screen appears in the spoken content or user-provided brand facts. No
invented decimals, no illustrative percentages, no placeholder axes. A viewer
who catches one fake number distrusts every real one (charter dimension 6 is
spent in a single frame).

### See, then hear — build with the speech

Bring the visual on slightly before the words that describe it, so the viewer
has the shape when the meaning arrives. Build multi-part data in steps timed
to the speaker's enumeration — the bars appear as the speaker counts them,
not all at once before or after. A chart that completes before the sentence
starts is a spoiler; one that completes after is an echo.

### The relation is the content

Chart form follows the claim's relation: comparison → side-by-side scale;
trend → a line with time; composition → parts of a whole; threshold → a
marked line the value crosses. If the claim has no relation — a single number
— the honest device is the number itself, large and typographically composed,
not a one-bar chart. Chart chrome (grids, axes, legends) is subordinated:
show the minimum scaffolding the relation needs.

### A static frame beats a weak animation

If the data moment cannot be animated meaningfully in the time the speech
allows, hold it as a designed still. Bad animation costs more than no
animation — motion draws the eye, and an eye drawn to a jerky counter is an
eye taken from the argument.

*Anchors:* one claim per data moment; the developed hold lasts long enough to
read every visible number aloud; count-up animations only when the magnitude
itself is the point.

## Demonstration and screen-capture moments

### The demo is the hero — packaging yields

When real product footage, a screen recording, or a physical demonstration is
on screen, it is the proof. The packaging retreats to annotation: the
strongest treatment is usually a clean reframe with generous scale, a single
callout, and the caption. Competing with a demo for attention inverts the
proof hierarchy — the film says "look at this" while pointing elsewhere.

### Annotate one thing at a time

A callout on a demo marks exactly one region or action per beat, timed to the
moment the speech references it (see-then-hear applies). Multiple simultaneous
callouts split the single thread of attention (motion-language rule: ≤2
things moving, and the demo itself is already one of them).

### Respect the demo's own time

Screen recordings carry their own causality — a click causes a response.
Never scale, crop, or speed-ramp a demo such that cause and effect separate
or fall out of frame. If the demo is slow, cut it honestly (a visible jump)
rather than silently accelerating through the wait: viewers read silent
speed-ups as hiding something.

*Anchor:* while a demo runs, at most one annotation layer plus captions; the
source stays ≥ two-thirds of the frame unless the annotation moment is itself
the declared focal point.
