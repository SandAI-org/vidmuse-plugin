# Try-on UGC

## Viewer expectation

Let the audience judge how an actual item looks or behaves when worn: styling,
silhouette, fit, scale, movement, and choice. Select the relevant dimensions;
glasses, a dress, shoes, and headphones require different evidence. A try-on
need not begin with a box, an offscreen question, or an unstyled "before".

## Concept and hook

Start from a specific style question, occasion, choice, visible contrast, or
creator reaction. The first frame can already show the item worn when the
interest is in how to style it or which option to choose. Use an unworn opening
when putting it on and revealing the result genuinely supplies the payoff.

Possible mechanisms include an occasion-based outfit decision, two meaningful
styling choices, an unexpected detail revealed on the body, or a friend's
reaction. These are options, not mandatory scripts. Explain why the difference
matters to this audience. Merely promising "same outfit, watch this" and adding
an accessory may be too predictable to sustain interest.

## Evidence and camera

Choose evidence from the product and concept:

- Garment: whole silhouette plus relevant fabric, length, waist, sleeve or
  neckline detail; a natural walk, turn, bend or reach where useful.
- Eyewear: correct face scale, front and temple views, clear ear/nose contact,
  and relation to the outfit. Reflections should follow the actual setting.
- Shoes/accessories: useful relation to body/outfit and relevant close details.
- Wearable technology: correct placement and actual use; technical benefits
  require separate evidence rather than assumed reactions.

A full-body hold helps assess silhouette. Reframing or a cut reveals a detail;
following a walk shows motion; a small turn reveals a side previously hidden.
State that purpose. Do not drift around a stationary model just to add energy.
The photographer may contribute a question or reaction if the concept calls
for it; they are not required. Fixed rear-camera shots are also valid.

Preserve the actual product, creator, wardrobe, light, and comparison conditions
where needed. A same-outfit comparison should not quietly change styling,
posture, body proportions, or light to manufacture the result. If showing
putting-on, maintain real contact and show enough action to establish that it
was actually worn, rather than appearing magically between frames.

## Speech, pacing, and close

Speak about visible specifics and this creator's choice. Reactions should
respond to the revealed detail; do not substitute repeated "obsessed" or
"so cute" for product evidence. Separate scripted preferences from assertions
of long-term personal use. Do not invent comfort, fit measurements, size advice,
or functional performance from appearance alone.

Allow enough time to inspect the worn result and relevant detail. Trim a
repetitive donning sequence if the choice or styling is the main interest;
retain it when the requested demonstration depends on it. Determine duration
from the concept rather than a fixed before/after split.

Match CTA to the objective: explore the actual style/color, choose an option,
visit a supplied destination, or answer a styling question when engagement is
the goal. Do not invent variants or a purchase offer.

## Review

Is the look readable? Is the comparison or choice meaningful? Does the item
match the product reference while worn? Does each view contribute useful
information? Is the payoff stronger than simply seeing the item appear?
