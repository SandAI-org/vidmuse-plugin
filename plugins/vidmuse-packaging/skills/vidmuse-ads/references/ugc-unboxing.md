# Unboxing UGC

## Viewer expectation

The audience follows a discovery: opening, contents, a notable detail, a first
reaction, and optionally first use. Interest can come from anticipation,
inspection, tactile/sound detail, or resolving a specific product question.
Do not impose a fashion transformation, photographer interview, or generic
product monologue onto every unboxing.

## Concept and hook

Choose what is worth discovering. Examples: a specific detail to inspect,
what is actually included, a creator's reason for ordering, an unusual reveal,
or a satisfying opening action. Establish the actual promise immediately;
"Let's unbox this" alone is weak when there is no interesting visual or question.

Do not manufacture purchase history, scarcity, surprise extras, value claims,
or disappointment. A scripted reveal can express the concept without pretending
to document a real consumer purchase or unsupported product experience.

## Inputs and opening state

Exact packaging and included items require supplied evidence. A product-only
photo does not establish the shipping box, retail package, protective materials,
accessories, or layout. Resolve missing material facts when authentic unboxing
depends on them; otherwise choose a supported product-reveal or first-look
concept and label it accurately. Do not invent a branded box to fill the gap.

Establish the starting state: closed, partly opened, or opening already underway.
State what is inside from the actual brief and maintain those objects across
shots. Do not reset the package or make components appear twice.

## Reveal, evidence, and camera

Select viewpoint for the task:

- Operator/top-down tabletop view: hands, opening mechanics, and contents.
- Angled table view: depth, lifting an item out, and close inspection.
- Creator-facing view: meaningful facial response or interaction, when needed.

The phone's rear-camera viewpoint must be physically clear. Top-down hands-only
unboxing need not add a face shot or even a newly generated character sheet.
Create only references required for the chosen concept; if a visible performer
is required, use the white identity-sheet contract.

Time each reveal to give new information. Retain the opening action, then
show contents, the selected detail, and first use if relevant. Cut between
mechanics and reaction with a reason; do not make every piece of packaging an
equally long shot. Hands must manipulate real closures and objects with coherent
contact, weight, orientation, and chronological state changes.

## Sound, speech, and close

Opening, paper, fabric, lid, or component sounds can carry an ASMR concept.
Speech may be brief, intermittent, voiceover, or absent. Do not force continuous
dialogue over a sound-led reveal. Conversely, preserve a reaction-led concept's
voice instead of imposing silence. Decide music and caption strategy from the
brief and editing needs.

Anchor reactions in a specific observed detail. Avoid calling ordinary contents
"insane" or adding fake astonishment. Show the detail that supports the reaction.
Unboxing can lead into try-on or demonstration, but identify that transition
and give it time; it is not automatically a complete performance review.

Close after the promised discovery has been delivered. Match CTA to the
actual objective and destination, such as viewing the supplied product, asking
which detail to test next, or proceeding to use. Never invent accessories,
variants, prices, discounts, or links.

## Review

Does the reveal provide a reason to watch? Are packaging and contents supported
by the inputs? Can the viewer follow opening, lifting, inspection and first use?
Do sound and reactions belong to those actions? Did the video actually deliver
the promised discovery rather than skip it?
